# Product Story

## Objective

Explain how ADF’s idea started, what problems were observed, why existing tools
felt fragmented, and why ADF was created.

## Background

Modern software teams increasingly rely on AI assistants. Productivity gains are
real, but so are process failures: lost context, undocumented decisions, and
incompatible tool stacks.

## Analysis

Observed pattern:

1. Architecture lives in chat or slides, not in the repository.
2. Each AI session restarts from incomplete context.
3. Documentation lags code; agents amplify the drift.
4. IDEs, generators, chat tools, and ticketing systems remain disconnected.
5. Enterprises cannot explain *how* AI-assisted work was governed.

Existing tools excel at generation. Fewer tools own **continuity, contracts, and
structure** as the product center.

## Strategy

Create ADF as a framework that treats repository knowledge, locked architecture,
cumulative builds, and human-led collaboration as the foundation—then layer Core,
Studio, marketplace, and governance on top.

## Recommendations

1. Tell the product story as continuity and structure—not “more AI magic.”
2. Use RC1 capabilities as proof of the story (Studio, collaboration, orchestration planning, governance views).
3. Keep fragmentation diagnosis factual; avoid competitor disparagement.

## Summary

ADF was created because AI-assisted development was productive but structurally
fragile. The product story is the move from fragmented sessions and tools to a
repository-native framework for durable AI engineering practice.
