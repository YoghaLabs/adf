# Problem Statement

## Objective

Define the industry problems ADF is designed to address, in language suitable
for executives and institutional buyers.

## Background

AI coding assistants are widely adopted. The constraint is no longer “can AI
write code?” but “can organizations trust, resume, and govern AI-assisted work?”

## Analysis

### Context fragmentation
Project truth is scattered across chats, tickets, wikis, and local notes. Agents
and humans restart from incomplete pictures.

### AI inconsistency
Without shared contracts and state, successive sessions produce divergent
assumptions and undocumented changes.

### Knowledge loss
Decisions evaporate when conversations end. Architecture rationale is hard to
recover.

### Poor documentation
Documentation is treated as optional. AI then amplifies undocumented systems.

### Vendor dependency
Critical process knowledge sits inside proprietary chat histories and closed
workflows, weakening portability and auditability.

### Disconnected tools
IDE, generator, marketplace, runtime, and review tools rarely share one
operating model.

## Strategy

Treat these as structural problems—not training gaps—and answer them with
repository-native SSOT, locked architecture, and explicit collaboration /
governance models.

## Recommendations

1. Lead enterprise conversations with this problem set.
2. Map each problem to a shipped ADF surface where accurate (docs, Studio, collaboration, governance).
3. Do not claim problems are “solved forever”; claim they are addressed by design.

## Summary

ADF targets fragmentation, inconsistency, knowledge loss, weak documentation,
vendor lock-in of process context, and disconnected tooling—the failure modes
that appear after AI coding speed improves.
